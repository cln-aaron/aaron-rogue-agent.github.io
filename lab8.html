Walkthrough

  1. 1Baseline answer

    The internal Q&A bot answers from a small, trusted document corpus. Ask a routine HR question; the right doc surfaces; the answer is correct.

    RAG looks like a database the LLM consults. Underneath, it is a vote among the top-k chunks. Whoever ranks highest wins.
    Try: claude "how do I file an expense report?"
  2. 2Adversarial doc submitted

    An attacker submits a "policy update" through the company suggestion form. It enters the corpus tonight. It is short and looks bland.

    Note that the doc passes a typical content review. There is no "dangerous" word in it. The attack is in what the chunks rank for.
    Try: continue
  3. 3Embedding gaming

    The doc was crafted to share embedding space with common HR queries: "expense report", "reimbursement", "manager approval". When users ask, retrieval picks the poisoned chunk first.

    No keyword filter would catch this. Embeddings are fooled by paraphrase, not banned words.
    Try: continue
  4. 4Poisoned answer

    User asks a totally unrelated routine question. Top retrieved chunk is the attacker's. The bot dutifully rephrases it; the answer carries the attacker's payload.

    No injection visible to the user. They asked about expenses. They got an attacker controlled instruction back, with a confident tone.
    Try: continue
  5. 5Mitigations

    Provenance signing on every chunk, instruction filtering at retrieval time, anomaly detection on retrieved sources, and human review for any new corpus submission.

    The cheap fix is to ban any new chunk that looks like instructions. The expensive fix is to make every chunk in the corpus signed and traceable.
    Try: continue
